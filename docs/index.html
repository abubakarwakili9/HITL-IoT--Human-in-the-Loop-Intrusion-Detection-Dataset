Beyond Intrusion Detection: Toward Trustworthy AI Decisions in IoT Cybersecurity

A reproducible benchmark for intrusion detection, calibration, selective deferral and human–AI oversight. It tests not only whether AI can identify cyberattacks, but when its predictions should be trusted, reviewed or deferred.

From detection to oversight

Four research areas follow a security decision from the first alert to the final call. Each has a fixed protocol and metric, so results from different papers can be compared directly.

  1. 1

    Intrusion detection

    Evaluate detectors across twelve IoT attack families with consistent features and fixed evaluation splits.

    Macro-F1, MCC, AUROC

  2. 2

    Trustworthy AI and calibration

    Measure predictive uncertainty and whether a model's confidence matches how often it is right.

    ECE, Brier score

  3. 3

    Selective deferral

    Decide when to accept a prediction and when to send it for review under a fixed budget.

    Errors captured at 8% review

  4. 4

    Human–AI oversight

    Compare review routing and intervention policies with simulated reviewer profiles, counting fixes against new mistakes.

    Corrections minus harms

Why HITL-IoT matters

High detection accuracy does not show whether a model's predictions are reliable, how uncertainty should shape a security decision, or when extra oversight actually helps. HITL-IoT makes those questions measurable.

With HITL-IoT, researchers can

  • evaluate intrusion detection across diverse IoT attack scenarios
  • measure model confidence, calibration and uncertainty
  • test selective prediction and learning-to-defer strategies
  • compare alert prioritisation and oversight policies under a fixed review budget
  • weigh the errors an intervention corrects against the ones it introduces

Research informed by international frameworks

EU AI Act, Article 14
Human oversight of high-risk AI: when people should interpret, intervene in or override a system's output.
NIST AI RMF 1.0
Measuring reliability and managing AI risk, through its Govern, Map, Measure and Manage functions.
NIST CSF 2.0
Detection and alert prioritisation as part of the Detect, Respond and Govern functions.
OECD AI Principles
Transparency, robustness and accountability in human-centred AI.

Research scope: HITL-IoT uses generated network flows and simulated oversight profiles. It supports experiments on the questions these frameworks raise; it does not certify compliance or measure real analysts.

What is in the benchmark

One week of smart-home traffic from five kinds of device, with twelve attack families spread across four threat groups and fixed train, validation and test splits. Every label is exact, every file is checked against a published SHA-256 hash, and the whole release can be rebuilt byte for byte.

Beyond detection, 10,227 flows carry review metadata: a decision, confidence, latency and written rationale from three configurable review profiles. That turns deferral, routing and correct-versus-harm questions into experiments anyone can repeat and compare.

Built for controlled experiments

  • Exact ground truth. Traffic is produced by a seeded simulation, so labels are known by construction rather than inferred after the fact.
  • Tunable difficulty. Each attack family's similarity to normal traffic is set explicitly, so hard cases are there on purpose.
  • Repeatable oversight. Review decisions come from configurable profiles, so a routing policy can be tested thousands of times at no cost.
  • Privacy-free. No real network, household or person is behind any record.

It is designed to sit alongside capture-based datasets such as CICIoT2023 and ToN-IoT: they test detection on real traffic, HITL-IoT tests what happens after detection.

False-alarm rates stay below 0.7% for every model, but false-negative rates range from 10.2% to 23.2%. The models rarely cry wolf; they do miss attacks. That asymmetry is what the oversight tasks study.

What the oversight experiments found

  • Missed attacks look confident. Reviewing the least-confident 8% of test flows catches 15.2% of the Random Forest's errors after calibration.
  • Calibration fixes the probabilities, not the misses. Isotonic calibration cuts expected calibration error from 0.0186 to 0.0014.
  • Two kinds of "uncertain" barely overlap. The generator's ambiguity and the model's own uncertainty agree almost at chance (AUROC 0.503, Spearman ρ 0.053).
  • Blind override does harm. Letting review profiles overrule every reachable prediction corrects 7 errors and introduces 293. A confidence gate prevents this.

Datasheet

Documentation in the seven-part format of Datasheets for Datasets (Gebru et al.).

Motivation

Most IoT intrusion datasets provide traffic features and labels only, so they cannot test what happens after a prediction: deferral, review routing, calibration and the trade-off between correcting and harming decisions. HITL-IoT provides a controlled, repeatable setting for those questions, relevant to human-oversight requirements such as Article 14 of the EU AI Act. Created by Abubakar Wakili (Euromed University of Fez), Muhammad Idris (University of Passau) and Sara Bakkali (Euromed University of Fez). No specific funding was received.

Composition

127,845 flow records with 60 columns: time, addressing, ports and services, device identity, volume and rate statistics, per-device rolling statistics, labels (is_attack, attack_type), construction signals and oversight-profile fields. 85.0% benign and 15.0% attack across twelve attack families. The oversight subset has 10,227 records: 8,160 high, 1,557 medium and 510 low competence profiles. Addresses are synthetic; the dataset contains no personal data.

Collection process

Produced in a fully controlled simulation of a smart home: 168 hours of traffic from 15 to 21 January 2024 across twelve devices. Each attack family has an explicit port-mimicry rate and similarity to normal traffic, so difficulty is known and adjustable. Labels are exact by construction, the release is byte-reproducible (seed 42), and no real network, household or person is involved.

Preprocessing and labelling

Labels are exact by construction. The oversight subset is the 8% of flows with the highest ambiguity score. Version 1.1 is leakage-audited: fields that could reveal the label are removed, and a published feature policy separates the 39 detection features from construction signals, so a high score has to be earned from traffic behaviour.

Uses

Leakage-safe IDS benchmarking, probability calibration, selective prediction, learning to defer, review routing, correction-versus-harm analysis, and teaching. Review profiles are configurable stand-ins for analysts, which makes oversight policies cheap to compare; studies of real analyst behaviour need human participants. For detection on real traffic, pair HITL-IoT with capture-based datasets such as CICIoT2023 and ToN-IoT.
